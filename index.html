What has to be in place before AI can be trusted with real engineering work

You can already build pipelines, models and apps. This track teaches the other half: designing the system around them so that what the AI produces is secure, verified, approved by the right person, affordable at scale and traceable years later. That is the difference between an engineer who makes something work and an architect who makes something that keeps working when a hundred teams and thousands of users depend on it.

The examples come from one reference design: a governed platform where engineering teams add their own tasks and apps, AI agents read documents and propose values, and people approve what becomes authoritative. You do not need that platform. You need to understand why each of its parts exists, because any system that does this kind of work needs the same parts.

How to use this track

  • Work through the five stages in order. Each module takes 45 to 90 minutes, plus outside study.
  • Press Play on the diagrams. Each one walks through a real flow step by step, with a log you can reread.
  • Write your own answer to every question before you open the model answer. Your notes stay in this browser.
  • The scenario questions have no single right answer. Compare your reasoning, your trade-offs and what you chose to give up.
  • Mark a module done when you can explain its diagram to someone else without looking.

Eight questions an architect asks of every design

You will meet these again at the end of every module. Learn them by heart. When you review any design, yours or someone else's, walk through all eight.

Identity. Who is asking, on whose behalf, and can the tool ever do more than that person?
Write path. How many ways can data change, and where is each one checked?

Stage 0 · Orientation · Module 0 · about 60 minutes

Think like an architect

An engineer is judged by whether the thing works. An architect is judged by whether the next ten things can be built on it without breaking it, by people who were not in the room. That changes what you optimise for: boundaries over features, decisions written down over decisions remembered, and proof over promise.

You will be able to

  • State the few invariants a platform must keep proving, and design checks for them
  • Write a decision record with context, alternatives, consequences and how it is verified
  • Separate what is proven, what is built on stubs and what is still missing, and say so plainly

Before you start

  • Pick a system you have built. Keep it in mind; every question in this module is about it.
  • Read one public architecture decision record collection (search "ADR examples", Michael Nygard's original format).

1. Invariants, not features

The reference design keeps proving three things on every change:

  • Adding an app never changes the architecture. An app is one folder. If an app needs a platform change, the platform is wrong.
  • New information becomes authoritative only through governance. AI produces claims, never facts.
  • The system learns only under governance. Agents propose, people approve, and a fixed mistake becomes a test.

Every later design choice serves one of these. When you cannot say which invariant a component protects, question whether it is needed.

2. Decisions are written, with their alternatives

An architecture decision record (ADR) holds the context, the decision, the alternatives considered and what each would cost, the rationale, the consequences you accept, and the check that verifies it.

The alternatives section is where architects show their thinking. A decision with no rejected alternative was not a decision; it was a default.

3. Proof over promise

Every claim about the system points at a check that runs on every change, or says that it does not. Three words keep you honest:

  • Proven a check in CI demonstrates it on every change
  • Built it runs, on a stub or synthetic data
  • Partial part is proven and the gap is named

Three design rules that follow

  • Configuration over code. A team adds mostly declarative files the platform reads, so the platform does not grow code per team.
  • Contracts over conventions. Every boundary (a raw record, an action request, an audit event, a tool's access level) has a written contract that CI checks and the runtime enforces.
  • Fail closed. A missing fact fails a rule. A config file that does not parse stops the system instead of being skipped. An unknown risk level is treated as high.

How the architect spends a day

  • Finds the person who owns a rule and gets it confirmed, instead of guessing it
  • Turns an open question into a named owner, a default assumption and the file where the answer will go
  • Measures before deciding: cost per run, reviewer minutes per value, share of data verified
  • Says what is not built, in the same document as what is

Architect scenarios

ScenarioA team lead says: "Our app is special. We just need one extra field in the shared ontology and one if-statement in the shared approval service. It is a two-line change." What do you do?

Do not merge the two lines. Ask which invariant they would break: an app that needs a platform change means either the platform is missing a general capability, or the team's need belongs in its own slot.

Separate the two cases. If the field is a concept only this team uses, it is an extension in their app folder, which they own and change freely. If other teams will need it too, it is a core change: version it, have the ontology owners review it, and make it available to everyone. The if-statement is the real warning sign. Shared services must never name an app; express the team's rule as configuration the service reads (a rule file, an approval tier), so the service stays the same for everyone.

Then make it a check, not a conversation: a guardrail in CI that fails when an app id appears in shared code, so the next team cannot do it by accident.

ScenarioManagement asks for a status slide: "Is the platform ready?" Everything works on synthetic data. Nothing is connected to the real identity provider or source systems. How do you answer?

Split the answer by kind of evidence. The logic is real and proven on every change: the gates, the write path, the access rules, the audit trail. The connections are built and tested against stubs that speak the same protocols. What is missing is other people's systems and decisions: a test tenant, access to source systems, owners who confirm the rules, legal review.

Then turn that into an ask with owners: who must provide each missing piece, and a pilot of limited scope that turns "built" into "proven". Never round "built on stubs" up to "ready"; the trust you lose the first time is hard to win back.

ScenarioYou are asked to choose between four ways to organise AI work across a hundred teams: each team builds its own tool; one central team builds everything; a shared code base with boundaries agreed by convention; or a fixed skeleton with one slot per team, checked in CI. Make the case for one, including what it costs.

For many teams on shared data, the fixed skeleton with slots. Identity, audit, approval and agent control then exist once instead of a hundred times, provenance has one answer across domains, and the boundary holds when people change because CI checks it.

Be explicit about the cost: platform work up front before the second team arrives, a platform team to run it, and teams must fit the contract or wait for a contract change. Also say where each alternative still fits: a team's own tool for a quick, isolated, low-risk gain; a central team for a few apps early on; convention-based sharing for short-lived prototypes. An architect who cannot say where the rejected options are right has not understood them.

Stage 1 · Security foundations · Module 1 · about 90 minutes

Identity and delegated access: an agent is never more than its person

Every request is authenticated before anything else happens, and the roles in the person's token are the only roles the system uses. When an AI agent works for a person, it carries that person's rights, on that person's projects, and nothing more.

You will be able to

  • Explain OIDC sign-in and what a resource server must check on every token
  • Design roles from directory groups, with project access as attributes (RBAC plus ABAC)
  • Design on-behalf-of delegation for agents, and separate delegated from service identities

Key terms

  • OIDC, OAuth 2.0: sign-in and delegated authorisation standards
  • Issuer, audience, expiry, algorithm: what a token must prove
  • On-behalf-of: the person is the subject, the agent is the actor
  • Group overage: a person in too many groups for the token to list

What the gateway checks on every token

  • Signature and algorithm. RS256 with a key from the issuer's key set. Refuse none and any algorithm you did not expect.
  • Issuer and audience. The token was issued by your identity provider, for your API, not for another application.
  • Expiry and not-before. And times that are numbers, not text.
  • Token type. A person's access token carries a delegated scope. An ID token or an application's own token must not sign a person in, and neither may a token from another tenant.
  • Shape. A list where a string belongs, or roles that are not a list of text, is refused with 401, not half-read later.

Roles and projects come only from groups

Directory groups map to roles (DomainEngineer, Approver, TechnicalAuthority, DataSteward, ReleaseManager, AgentOwner) and to projects (one group per project). Access is the combination: role-based checks on what you may do, attribute-based checks on which project, which classification and which purpose.

A person in more groups than the token can list arrives with no group list at all. That sign-in must be refused with a reason, never read as "no groups", or you silently downgrade or, worse, misroute people.

The ruleAn agent acting for a person gets an on-behalf-of token: the person is the subject, the agent is the actor, and the grant is what that person's roles allow, on that person's projects only. A read-only user's agent may only read. Service agents that no person drives run under their own narrow identity with no person's roles. An agent's token can never be used to sign in as a person, and a service token can never pass as a person's.

Architect scenarios

ScenarioA project manager asks that their assistant agent may approve values "on their behalf" while they are on holiday, since they are an approver anyway. Delegated access would technically allow it. What do you design?

Refuse approval by any agent, delegated or not. Delegation answers "may this agent read and propose for this person"; it must never extend to the decisions that make data authoritative. An approval is a human judgement with a name on it, and the audit trail must show a person made it. In the reference design no agent can approve, not even on someone's behalf, and the write path checks that the approver is a person with the role.

Solve the real problem instead: a deputy approver in the same role and project, review queues with escalation timers so nothing waits unseen, and a risk-tiered approval where low-consequence values with measured accuracy may pass a controlled workflow (Module 8).

ScenarioThe service grows to tens of thousands of users. Some senior engineers belong to more than 200 directory groups, and their sign-ins arrive without a group list. Someone proposes "just treat missing groups as the default role". Evaluate.

That fails open in disguise: a person's real access is replaced by a guess, which is either too little (they are blocked from their projects) or too much (if the default ever grows). Refuse the sign-in with a clear reason now, and fix it at the source: assign only the relevant groups to the application so they always fit in the token, or use application roles instead of groups. Write the choice down as an open question for the identity team, with "refuse with reason" as the safe assumption until they answer.

ScenarioAn ingress proxy sits in front of your API. Every unauthenticated request now appears to come from the proxy's address, so one attacker exhausts the anonymous rate limit for everyone. How do you fix it without trusting a header an attacker can forge?

Name the trusted proxies explicitly in configuration. Read the caller's address from the forwarded header only when the request arrived from one of those proxies, and take the last hop they added, not the first value (which the caller controls). Count refused tokens against that address too, so a flood of forged tokens is rate limited. Until the proxy addresses are known, write it down as an open question with the current behaviour as the known risk.

Stage 1 · Security foundations · Module 2 · about 75 minutes

One write path: the only way data changes

If data can change in five places, you have five places to secure, audit and keep consistent. A governed system has one service through which every change goes, for every app, person and agent. It checks the same things in the same order, inside one transaction, and only then writes a new version with an audit event.

You will be able to

  • List the ordered checks a write path must make, and say why the order matters
  • Explain why a caller must never supply the facts its own request is judged by
  • Design responses that leak nothing about other projects' data (no oracles)

Key terms

  • Payload contract: only the action's declared inputs are accepted
  • Idempotency key: the same request twice changes nothing the second time
  • Oracle: a response that reveals something you may not read
  • Versioned write: data is never updated in place

Try it: send a request and watch which check stops it

Pick a request above. The checks run left to right; the first one that fails stops the request, and nothing is written.

Why each check is where it is

  • Contract and access first: cheap, and a refusal here reveals nothing.
  • Payload contract: a payload holds only the action's inputs. Facts the rules judge come from the stored object, governed reference data and computed facts inside the platform, never from the caller. Otherwise the caller writes their own exam answers.
  • Project scope: an object of another project answers exactly as one that does not exist, at every step, so nobody can probe which ids exist.
  • Evidence: every evidence id must resolve to a real line of a stored source document. Made-up evidence is refused.
  • Idempotency: the same request delivered twice, even to two replicas at once, writes once.
  • Business rules, compliance, open conflicts, approvals: last, because they need everything above to be true.

What security testing found here

Real findings from repeated security rounds on the reference design, each now a test:

  • A filtered list counted matches in other projects: an oracle for their ids and values.
  • The action endpoint told an object of another project from a missing one.
  • A person could confirm a value they had set themselves, certifying their own data.
  • A write could cite another person's approval id.
  • Governance objects (claims, conflicts) had no project, so another project's member could reject them.

None was a crash. Each was a logic gap in what the write path believed. That is where most damage in these systems happens.

The ruleAgents get no direct database write access. Only one execution identity may act, only through the write path, and only after an approval exists. Data is never edited in place: a wrong value is superseded by a new version that passes the same checks.

Architect scenarios

ScenarioAn integration team wants to bulk-load 40,000 legacy records straight into the database "because the write path is too slow for a migration". What do you allow?

Not a direct load. A side door used once becomes the path everyone uses under deadline pressure, and those records would look as trustworthy as approved ones.

Load them through the same path, in batches, as migration records. They enter labelled unverified, with the migration as their source and the legacy system as evidence. They are usable on operational screens with their label, but advanced apps and exports see only verified values (Module 11), and engineers verify them over time through tasks. If speed really is a problem, measure it and fix the path; a slow write path for 40,000 records is a performance bug, not a reason for a bypass.

ScenarioFour gateway replicas receive the same "create object" request at the same moment, each with a different idempotency key because the client generated new keys on retry. What must hold, and how?

Exactly one object must exist afterwards. Idempotency keys alone do not save you when the client changes them, so the uniqueness must also be on the business key (the object's identity) inside the store, with the check and the write in one transaction. Three replicas then fail the uniqueness check and answer as a duplicate. Prove it with a race test against the real database, not with mocks, and include it in CI.

ScenarioA request runs out of time in the gateway, but the write path is still processing it. The client retries. What should the design guarantee?

A request past its deadline can no longer write: the write path checks the deadline before committing. The retry then carries the same idempotency key, so whichever attempt commits first wins and the other is a no-op. The client gets a clear answer either way. Without the deadline check you get writes nobody is waiting for; without idempotency you get two.

Proving security: tests that try to break the system

A gate that has never been seen to refuse is not evidence of anything. Security in an architect's sense is a set of checks that run on every change: a threat model, hostile requests, random sequences of real requests, faults injected in the middle of a step, and the gates themselves broken on purpose to see if anything notices.

You will be able to

  • Write a threat model as threat, control and proof
  • Choose between an attack suite, fuzzing, stateful fuzzing, fault injection and mutation testing
  • Make an audit trail tamper-evident

Key terms

  • Trust boundary: where data or control crosses from less to more trusted
  • Mutation testing: break the code on purpose; a test must fail
  • Invariant: what must hold after every step
  • SBOM: the bill of materials of your software supply chain

Architect scenarios

ScenarioA mutation run shows that breaking "an agent may not approve its own proposal" is caught by no test. The release is due tomorrow. What do you do?

Stop the release of anything that touches that path until a test catches the mutant. A surviving mutant on a security control means the control could be removed tomorrow by an innocent refactor and nobody would know. Writing the test is usually an hour; shipping without it means your safety net has a known hole in exactly the place that matters most.

Then ask the systemic question: why did the suite miss it? Usually refusals are under-tested compared with approvals. Add a rule that every scenario carries negative checks.

ScenarioA database administrator could, in principle, rewrite audit events. Legal asks whether the audit trail would hold up in a dispute. What do you design, and what do you not claim?

Chain every event's hash in sequence order and keep the head of the chain outside the database, in anchors written once to a store the database administrator cannot change (for example a write-once folder only the anchoring job may create files in). A verify job then detects a changed, deleted or inserted event or a rewritten anchor.

Do not claim it prevents tampering. It makes tampering evident. Also state the remaining questions: who runs the anchoring job, how often, where anchors live, and who reads the verification result.

Stage 2 · Cloud and architecture · Module 4 · about 75 minutes

A fixed skeleton with one slot per team

To scale to many teams, build what is the same for everyone once, and give each team one place to put what is unique to them. The shared part never names a team. The team's part never edits the shared part. And a machine checks both, on every change, so the rule does not depend on discipline.

You will be able to

  • Decide what belongs in the shared skeleton and what belongs in a team's slot
  • Design the checks that hold the boundary in place
  • Design ownership as data: one owning group per path, one deciding group per decision

Key terms

  • Internal developer platform: the platform team owns templates, contracts and runtime; teams own what they build on them
  • Leak check: no team's name in shared code
  • Add-app proof: generating a new app leaves shared code byte-identical
  • Core and extension: a locked shared model that teams extend

Built once, shared by every team

  • Identity, access and on-behalf-of access for agents
  • Raw layer, harmonisation, quarantine and run results
  • Claims, evidence and the gates
  • The single write path
  • Verification states, read purposes and engineer tasks
  • Compliance policies: data protection, AI regulation, contract terms
  • Agent patterns, the shared tool library, runtime agents
  • Audit, lineage, correlation ids, observability
  • The store, event backbone, durable workflows, model gateway

Unique, built by the team in its slot

  • Its object types, as extensions of the shared core
  • Its source mappings, unit maps and aliases
  • Its claim rules and the consequence level of each value
  • Its actions and workflow
  • Its own verification tasks
  • The contract terms of its own sources, and personal data in its own types
  • Its extraction profile and golden test set
  • Its skills, one file per task
  • Its team, on-call and release line

Exercise: skeleton or slot?

A new team wants to build an inspection app. For each item, decide where it belongs.

Six checks hold it in place

  • Leak check: no app id in shared folders
  • Add-app proof: CI generates a new app and checks shared files stay identical, and that editing shared code is rejected
  • App contract: manifest, team, release line, compliance terms, agent from a pattern
  • Ownership: code owners generated from one ownership file
  • Core lock: the shared model cannot change without a new version
  • Catalogue: every folder is marked executable or design reference

A locked core, extended by teams

The shared ontology is the language every app and agent uses: assets, tags, documents, revisions, materials, projects, claims, evidence, approvals. With one app it can be owned centrally; with a hundred it becomes the main point of contention.

So split it. The core is frozen at a version and reviewed by an ontology board. A new type or property is a minor version; a removed or changed one is breaking and needs a deprecation window. Teams extend it in their own slot and may never redefine a core type.

What it costs

The skeleton front-loads work: contracts, gates and guardrails exist before the second team. A team that does not fit the contract waits for a contract change. You need a platform team (in the reference design, 8 to 15 people at scale) and an ontology board.

In return, the boundary is a check, not an agreement, so it holds when people change. That is the trade an architect makes deliberately and explains.

Stage 2 · Cloud and architecture · Module 5 · about 60 minutes

Task functions, apps and models: three levels on the same path

Engineering work comes in three sizes. A task function is one job an engineer does by hand every week. An app combines several task functions into a result. Every skill follows the same chain

The rules that make a skill trustworthy

  • A value the source gave is never overwritten.
  • A field that cannot be derived by rule stays empty, so the row stops at the checks and goes to a person. Nothing is guessed.
  • Every row cites the source's own line as evidence.
  • The source must have contract terms, or the skill may not read it.
  • A generated document prints only verified values. A gap in a required field holds the document for a person.
  • A computed field (a sum, a band in a table, a yes or no comparison) is printed only when every field it reads was printed, and carries their evidence.

Hybrid cloud and placement: data stays where its owner allows

Not all data may go to the cloud. Some must stay on infrastructure the company controls; some may only be reached from inside a customer's or operator's own network. A good design separates the control plane (policy, governance, audit), the knowledge layer (raw data, ontology, evidence) and execution (agents, workflows, skills) from the compute underneath, so the same code and rules run in every zone and work can move without a rebuild.

Placement simulator

Choose a source's residency, its classification, the zone a platform instance runs in, and the work. The decision follows the same rules the reference design enforces, and it fails closed.

Three zones

  • Cloud: bought as a service. Little up-front cost; governance and data services included.
  • Company-controlled: colocation, a dedicated cluster or a hybrid offering such as Azure Local. A self-hosted model on owned GPUs.
  • Operator-local: an instance inside a customer's network for data they let you reach only from there. Nothing derived leaves.

Four enforcement points

  • The raw layer refuses and quarantines a record its zone may not hold
  • The model gateway refuses a call unless an allowed model endpoint is in the zone
  • Heavy compute is refused and the missing capacity reported
  • The export leaves out rows resting on such evidence

Only audit events without content cross zones, so the control plane keeps one trail. Values, evidence text and documents never cross.

The strategic question

When does owning compute beat buying it? When steady, heavy workloads (simulation, analysis, high-GPU models, training) cost more as a service than to own, or when data cannot leave company control. Owning servers and owning a building are separate decisions; the building comes last.

Because a well-designed system calls a model only at named judgement points, early stages run comfortably in the cloud. Decide from the system's own cost and capacity metrics.

Stage 3 · Applied AI and agents · Module 8 · about 90 minutes

Claims, not facts: how AI output becomes authoritative

A language model reading a datasheet produces something that looks like data. It is not data yet. It is a claim: a proposed value with a source line, an extractor version and a confidence. It becomes authoritative only by passing deterministic gates and the approval of the right person, and it loses that status the moment its source is superseded.

GateWhat it checksWhen it fails
1 ProvenanceThe claim points to a real segment of an approved source revisionRejected; never reaches a person
2 StructureThe claim follows the claim schemaRejected
3 Deterministic rulesType, unit and range per attribute; a missing fact failsQuarantined with the rule that failed
4 Cross-source checkCompares with the current value and the authority registerA conflict object for a person, never settled silently
5 Expert and policyConsequence tier, source authority, publication policy, then the tier's reviewersWaits in the discipline queue, or blocked by policy
6 PublishOnly an approved claim reaches the authoritative viewNot published
7 RevalidateWhen a source revision is superseded, every claim it supported is flaggedBack to gate 5

Gate 5 is risk-tiered

A named expert approving every claim works for six claims, not sixty thousand. Reviewer time is the main limit on growth, so the tier follows the consequence of an error:

  • Named high consequence (design values, quantities, ratings, register changes): a named technical authority
  • Single medium consequence, or low where a condition fails: one reviewer from the discipline queue
  • Auto low consequence and every condition holds: the controlled workflow, with 10 % sampled for audit

Auto needs confidence ≥ 0.95, rules passed, no open conflict, an approved revision, evidence, and extractor precision ≥ 0.95 measured on a labelled golden set, never reported by the extractor itself. Unknown consequence is high. A value a language model extracted is never auto-approved.

Claim lifecycle

DRAFT→EXTRACTED→VALIDATED→CONFLICT→PENDING_REVIEW→APPROVED→PUBLISHED→SUPERSEDED

A claim cannot skip a state. Nothing fails silently: a broken rule goes to quarantine with a category and a reason; a disagreement becomes a conflict object; a superseded revision sends its claims back; a value nobody checked is labelled unverified and becomes a task.

Reviewers see trust signals beside every claim: source authority, extractor version and confidence, which gates passed, the current value and its history, agreement with other sources, and past corrections of the same extractor.

Five runtime agents, one per boundary

Why these boundaries

  • Untrusted content. Only the Extractor reads document text. Keeping retrieval tools out of that context means an instruction hidden in a document cannot reach a query or a listing tool.
  • Delegated or service identity. A person's request never runs with service rights.
  • Change to data. The identity that decides never acts; the identity that acts never decides. Only the Executor holds action.

Supervised agents

  • Every agent and role has an owner, a risk class, a model and prompt version, an evaluation suite and a policy. An active agent without them may not run.
  • Every tool has a version, an access level within its pattern and a test. Shared tools live once in a library and are pinned by major version.
  • Every agent carries a fingerprint of its files. A changed agent is refused at run time until governance approves and restamps it.
  • Every audit event names the runtime agent and the role.

Token cost: the model is a tool, called only where judgement is needed

The token cost of a multi-agent system comes from the model orchestrating, not from the number of agents. Every model turn rereads its system prompt, tool schemas and the case so far. A chain of 113 tool calls driven by models costs hundreds of thousands of input tokens; the same chain run by a workflow costs one extraction call.

Cost calculator

The same planning model as the reference design's decision record. Call and handoff counts are measured on its flows; prompt, schema and context sizes are assumptions you can change. Prices are planning figures per million tokens (standard model 3 USD in and 15 out; small model 1 and 5; cache reads at 10 % of input).

The model boundary in numbers

  • One model step in the whole platform, named in policy (extract); any other step is refused
  • 29 of 31 reference flows make no model call at all; skills and rules call none
  • A token cap of 16,000 per call, counted on the whole request as sent
  • 60 calls a minute and 200 USD a month per app; a warning at 80 %; a platform ceiling of 50,000 USD
  • The model gets cleaned segments only, never the full document or other projects' data
  • A duplicate delivery is recognised and not run again; verified values are reused, not extracted again

Safe failure

A call the gateway refuses (over quota, data rules, unlisted step) or the model cannot answer (rate limited, overloaded, stopped at its token limit, refused, no tool call) does not stop the work. The app's deterministic rules read the document instead, the reason is kept, and the call is charged and alerted.

Retry rules matter: retry 408, 409, 429 and 5xx with backoff and the server's retry-after (capped), never retry a rejected request. Quotas live in the shared store, so every replica counts against the same budget.

Verified data: check against the system that owns it, then remember

Advanced apps built on unchecked data turn it into confident answers. So every tracked value carries a verification state worked out from its own history, every read declares a purpose, and data is trusted only after it has been compared with its authoritative source system. Only verified knowledge goes into the knowledge graph.

Systems of record are authoritative; copies never are

Each field names the system that wins for it, in what scope and at what rank, with an owner, a steward, a classification and how often it must be updated. Typical systems of record in engineering:

  • Engineering data hub: which tags exist (written first, then read back)
  • Tag and document register: tag registrations, certificate data
  • Project information system: project and study data
  • Maintenance and materials system: job cards, stock, reservations

A discipline's own working database (for example a metering database) is never authoritative. Nor is a lakehouse copy, an export, or the platform's own semantic layer: they are compared with the source, not trusted instead of it.

Four states, never set by hand

StateWhen

unverifiedSeed, migration or import data, or a write without evidence and approval

A value that changes loses its verification, because the new version must earn it again. Confirming a value never changes it, and you can never verify a value you set yourself.

Stage 4 · Where AI and security meet · Module 12 · about 90 minutes

The model boundary: one checkpoint around every model call

A model call is the riskiest step in the system: it is where data can leave, where hidden instructions can steer behaviour, and where cost can run away. So every call passes one boundary, in a fixed order, before the model sees anything. And what the model returns is treated as an untrusted proposal, never as a fact or a command.

The data guard, in order

  1. The call names its source
  2. Its classification is a known one; none given counts as confidential; restricted is never sent
  3. The zone's model endpoint is one the source's residency and the classification allow
  4. The source's contract terms allow model processing
  5. The step is named in policy, from a chain stage that declares it, within its token cap
  6. The app has quota left, counted in the shared store

Data rules run before the quota, so a refused call costs nothing and reveals nothing.

Prompt injection: defence in depth

  • A safety scan quarantines hidden instructions before any agent reads the document
  • The extractor and cross-checks get metadata and cleaned segments, never the full text with quarantined lines
  • No decide or action tool ever receives document content
  • The extractor has no retrieval or query tools to misuse
  • Output is a claim with a schema; it passes every gate and is never auto-approved

The first scan caught 10 of 25 ways of hiding an instruction. Reading each line as a person sees it (normalising invisible and look-alike characters, spacing, encodings, split lines, other languages) raised it to 25 of 25, and 20 of 20 on a set written afterwards.

Prompt logs

Every call is logged with secrets and personal data masked. Retention follows the classification: masked text kept for synthetic and internal data, only a hash and metadata for confidential, nothing beyond the audit event for restricted.

The assistant question

When people's own assistants want the platform's data (for example through an MCP server in front of the API), what a tool returns goes into the assistant's model, outside your gateway. Keep the same sign-in, project scope, read purposes and write path, and ask who approves which assistants.

Regulation

Under the EU AI Act a company using a provider's model is a deployer. Register every AI use with its risk class, human oversight, transparency and logging. Extraction whose output is used only after gates and a person is a limited-risk working assessment, pending legal review.

Stage 4 · Where AI and security meet · Module 13 · about 75 minutes

Governing agents and change: no single owner, four eyes, a ledger

The most dangerous change to an AI system is a change to the system itself: a prompt, a rule, a gate, an agent's tools. So no agent, profile, tool or skill has a single owner. Every change is classed by the files it touches, needs approval from people other than its author, and is written to a ledger outside the code host. Compliance is written as policy that CI checks and the write path enforces.

ClassWhat it touchesApprovals
0DocumentationNone beyond CI
1One app's own profile contentThe app team; may go straight through on green evaluations only if the app's agent is low risk
2A new or changed skill; any unknown fileThe governance group
3Privileged: gates, runtime agents, chains, model steps, access, the write pathTwo governance members plus a security reviewer

The rules

  • Nobody approves their own change
  • Approvals given before the last push do not count
  • Review within 3 working days
  • Break-glass merges without the missing approvals, is recorded and is reviewed within 2 working days
  • Every merged change writes a record once to a ledger outside the code host, by the pipeline's identity only

Compliance as policy

  • Data protection: only directory ids, never names, emails or phone numbers; every property that can hold personal data declared with purpose, lawful basis and retention; access requests answered from the system
  • AI regulation: every AI use registered; prohibited practices may not be built; model output always passes a person
  • Contract terms per source: may it enter the semantic layer, go to a model, leave the company, train a model

Learning under governance

Agents observe and propose. A person in the playbook's role approves. The change is applied on a sandbox copy, verified by every check, remembered as a lesson and turned into a permanent check. A value an expert corrects becomes a new evaluation case for the extractor that got it wrong.

A mistake is allowed once; after that it is a test.

Design traceability: every requirement points at the code that implements it and the check that proves it, with an honest status.

Capstone · about 3 hours

Capstone: design material availability as a capability

Now design one capability yourself, end to end, using every module. Write your answers before you open the reference design. You will be judged on the same things an architecture review would judge: where authority sits, who may do what, how it fails, what it costs, and what you still need from other people.

The brief

Engineers want to design with articles that are actually available, in company warehouses or at suppliers. Job cards are created and status-managed in the maintenance and materials system. A stock overview tool shows stock across portfolios and takes its data from that system, possibly through the data lake (not confirmed). Cross-portfolio simulations today run on first come, first served.

Downstream, a job card is not released for installation without full material coverage; a shortfall becomes a deviation in a coordination meeting. By then the article is chosen and most of the lead time is spent. So availability has to be considered at design time, when the engineer chooses the article.

Known: stock is not the same as available. The overview's quantity includes material reserved or allocated in other portfolios, blocked (quarantine, non-conformance, wrong or missing certificate) or owned by another customer. Unknown: the overview's interface, and whether the materials system can hold a reservation written by another system. The shared core has material, material line and work package types, but no stock, demand or reservation types.

Part 1 · AuthorityIs this one app's feature or a platform capability? Which system is authoritative for stock, reservations and job cards, and what role does a lakehouse copy play?

A platform capability. Every app with material demand (take-off, equipment list, equipment, metering, later piping and electrical) needs the same answer, and two apps computing availability their own way can promise the same stock twice. An app registers a material demand; it never computes availability or reserves.

The materials system and the stock overview stay the systems of record for stock, reservations and job cards. The platform reads them live and never treats a copy of a stock level as authoritative. The lakehouse and the ontology hold normalised material terms, historical decisions and relations, for analysis and traceability.

Part 2 · Agents and decisionsWhere, if anywhere, does a model belong? Which runtime identity computes availability, and which one reserves?

Candidate matching runs on the delegated Extractor: exact matches by material number are rules; equivalents by commodity, size, rating and grade are a named judgement point in the model-step policy, returned as a proposal the engineer must verify. The availability service runs on the Gatekeeper (decide, service identity) and never calls a model: available to promise = physical on hand − blocked − reserved − allocated, across every portfolio, returning Available, Partially available, Not available or Unknown. Reservation, release, allocation and job setting are action types run by the Executor through the write path. Letting an agent decide and reserve would put a model in a decision that needs the same answer every time, and give an identity that reads untrusted documents action access.

Part 3 · Concurrency and evidenceTwo portfolios ask for the last four units at the same moment. What happens, and what does the system store?

One first-come-first-served queue per stock position, where "first" is the time the write path accepted a valid reservation request. Exactly one reservation of four is created; the other is refused or partial. A reservation is refused or reduced if available to promise has dropped since the check it cites; simulations hold nothing; reservations expire; queue exceptions need a stated reason and an approval.

Every check is stored as evidence: request, source reference, source timestamp, calculation and result. Every later action cites it, so later you can answer why new material was ordered when stock existed, or why blocked stock was reserved.

Part 4 · Failure and phasingThe materials system cannot yet hold reservations from another system. What do you ship, and what do you never ship?

Ship read-only first: demand, candidates, the availability check, simulation and engineer verification, saying on every result that nothing is reserved. Reservations are written to the materials system first by the sync saga and confirmed by read-back; a reservation the system does not confirm leaves no platform reservation. A platform-owned reservation ledger as the system of record is acceptable only as an interim step with the gap shown on every result, because anything issued directly in the materials system can still take the stock. A check older than the freshness limit gives Unknown and blocks reservation. Never ship a reservation from a copy.

Part 5 · Questions for other peopleList the questions you cannot answer yourself, and who owns each.

  • Can the materials system hold a reservation written by another system, and through which interface? Can it hold one before a job card exists? (system owner)
  • What interface does the stock overview offer, and how fresh is it compared with the materials system? Does it expose certificate state, quarantine, ownership and portfolio per position? (its owner)
  • How are today's cross-portfolio simulations run? (domain expert)
  • Which rules govern reusing one project's or customer's surplus on another? (contracts, materials management)
  • Is supplier stock available as data, or only as quotes? (procurement)
  • What counts as covered at job-card release: reserved, allocated or on hand? (installation coordinator)
  • Who owns the availability rules? (a materials management role, to be named)

Part 6 · ProofWhich scenarios and guardrails must exist before anyone trusts it?

  • Two portfolios request the last four units at once: exactly one reservation of four
  • A stale check gives Unknown and blocks reservation
  • Blocked, uncertified and other-customer stock is never reported as available
  • A reservation not confirmed on read-back leaves no platform reservation
  • A job card whose requirements match design-time reservations reports full coverage without a second entry; an unmatched requirement becomes new demand
  • Guardrails: no app tool reads stock outside the capability; no tool of pattern extract, retrieve or advise can call the reservation actions
  • New core types as an additive minor version of the ontology, reviewed by the board
